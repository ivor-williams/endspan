Endspan Building a palliative and end-of-life care system that truly works for everyone

We are bringing together philanthropists who want to fund the changes the system needs but is not currently set up to deliver.

1

Dying has changed

The way we die has changed faster than the systems built to care for us

Understand the context →

People are living longer and dying more slowly. The predictable decline over months that the modern hospice movement was built around is being displaced by dementia, frailty and multimorbidity, where the final phase can last years. Care homes are on track to become the most common place of death in the UK by 2040, and by then roughly 150,000 more people will need palliative care than do today. The infrastructure has not kept up: district nurse numbers have fallen 43% since 2009.

2

Excellence already exists

There is more good care than there has ever been

Excellent hospice-at-home and community palliative care services are running in every region of the UK. Many care homes are doing good end-of-life work without being recognised or properly resourced for it. Outside the formal health system a new movement is growing, from death cafes and end-of-life doulas to compassionate communities. Social finance has shown that integrated community models can be handed over and sustained on mainstream NHS funding, and government policy is now pointing the same way.

3

But the system is stuck

Almost nothing connects what already works with what is emerging

Proven community models stay in the place that produced them. New approaches reach the end of a three-year grant before they can generate the evidence that would justify continuing them. The digital infrastructure that would let care follow a person across hospice, hospital, care home and home is largely unbuilt. There is a great deal to build, and no one is currently building it.

4

Funders are the missing piece

There is real generosity, but very little of it is aimed at changing how the system works

See where the money goes →

Around £22bn of public money is spent each year on people in their last year of life. Grant funding from trusts and foundations to UK palliative care comes to about £9.2m a year, in grants averaging £3,800, almost all of it aimed at keeping existing services running. What is missing is a strategic investor and coordinator: one able to connect what already works, back new approaches long enough to prove themselves, and build the shared infrastructure no single organisation can.

Good care is delivered by hospices, NHS community teams, GPs, care home chains, neighbourhood organisations and families, often at the same time. Endspan Phase 1 reportChapter 4

Philanthropic funding in palliative and end-of-life care

Prototype sample. This page carries pages 31 to 34 of the working report as written, to test the reading layout and the animated charts.

Chapter 3 showed where the system is stuck. This chapter asks what role money plays in keeping it there, and what a coalition's money could do differently. The coalition needs to know four things before it commits financially.

  • The first is scale. Around £22bn of public money is spent each year on people in their last year of life. Any philanthropic fund will be small by comparison, so it cannot replace public spending. Its value lies in changing how that spending is used - acting as first loss capital that can take risks on innovation that public funding cannot.
  • The second is where philanthropic money goes now, so our coalition can see the gaps and avoid duplicating what others already fund.
  • The third is what other funding instruments have achieved in this field, and where they have fallen short.
  • The fourth is what principles should guide a coalition's investment as a result.

We take these in turn. We begin with the money around the system: public spending, what households pay, and private capital. We then look at philanthropy in detail, drawing on a decade of grants data and the published accounts of 17 hospices, and at what social investment has taught the field. Our central finding is that almost all of this money sustains the system as it is or improves it at the margins. Very little backs the experiments that could change it. This missing middle is the space we think a coalition should fill. The chapter closes with the principles for doing that, and examples of funders elsewhere who have tried.

How end of life care is funded today

The funding model for end of life care - including how philanthropy currently works - is both a contributor to and condition of the failures in today's dominant end of life care system. For the system to change, funding models and philanthropic discipline also needs to change.

Funding for home-based end of life care comes predominantly from public funding and households' own money. Broadly speaking, philanthropic and money plays a key role in maintaining the hospice sector, venture capital is active in the care home sector, and outcomes-based social finance is enabling innovation in primary and secondary health care.

In this chapter we first look at overall financial flows in the space, before focusing in on the philanthropic funding landscape and two important social finance programmes. We aim to detail the principles by which new disciplined philanthropic investment could be deployed.

Public money

The primary source of funding for end of life care is public money. The Nuffield Trust and the Health Economics Unit estimate that around £22 billion of public money is spent on people in the last year of life in the UK39. This is roughly as much as the Home Office's entire budget, covering everything from policing to counter-terrorism to borders.40

Over half of this total expenditure (almost £12 billion) was on health care, 22% (almost £5 billion) was on social care, and 25% (£5.5 billion) was on social security like state pension payments.

The NHS spends £11.9bn on end of life healthcare annually. For comparison NHS maternity services are funded In 2021/22, overall spend on NHS maternity and neonatal care was around £4.9 billion.41

Within healthcare, the UK spends five times as much on supporting people in the final year of life as hospital inpatients, as we do supporting them with primary care, community health and hospice care. The vast majority of money is concentrated in emergency reactive care. Healthcare spending is nominally directed by Integrated Care Board commissioning decisions, though in practice much of it, particularly emergency and acute activity, is driven by national payment mechanisms and demand rather than by deliberate local choices.

For social care expenditure, the largest element is residential and nursing home care (63%), paid for by local authorities. Local authorities have a statutory duty to assess and arrange residential and nursing care for those with eligible needs, but they only fund it for people who fall below the means-test thresholds. Continuing Healthcare (CHC) is a package of care funded by the NHS. And the £170m 'other' covers things like the home adaptations and equipment.

Many of the dynamic loops described in Chapter 2 are tied to public funding models.

Household money

Individuals and their families also contribute both as charitable givers and as private customers within end of life care.

A key funding source for hospices is individual donations (e.g. money raised through donation campaigns and fun runs). Evidence from hospices UK shows that on average 27% of independent hospices' total income is from donations (£351m, though this figure also covers grants from trusts and foundations), and 18% is from legacies (£234m).42

Contribution as private customers is more challenging to calculate. However, the ONS estimates that 37% of care home residents are self-funders in England.43 LaingBuisson values the private pay market for care homes in the UK at £14.1 billion, though only part of this will be for end of life care.44

Venture and private finance

A third pool of capital circulates around end of life care. Though this is wider than end of life care it is a useful context. UK healthcare real estate attracted £12 billion of investment in 2025, around four times the five-year average, driven overwhelmingly by US REITs acquiring care home portfolios. Welltower alone deployed over £7 billion in the UK care sector that year.45 LaingBuisson values the wider UK care home market for older people at £27.3 billion,46 and the homecare and supported living market at a further £12.4 billion (this also includes care for all ages),47 much of it consolidated under private equity ownership.

There is some venture capital aimed more directly at death and dying, but has struggled to find a workable return: Farewill, the best known UK 'death tech' start-up, raised around £30-39 million from investors including Augmentum Fintech and Highland Europe, but was sold in 2024 for just £12.9 million.48 This illustrates some of the challenges for commercial capital in the case, and therefore the key role for philanthropy.

More developed commercial innovation pathways are also active within the medications, technologies and devices that support people at end of life.
